abstract: "Camera trapping has become a cornerstone of mammal monitoring, yet the volume of images frequently exceeds the capacity for manual review. Automated image-processing tools—like AI-based species classifiers and on-device alerting systems—offer scalable solutions, but uptake in applied research (field-based ecological studies) remains poorly characterised. We conducted a scoping review of AI in mammal camera-trapping studies (2014–2026), complemented by a corresponding-author survey, to assess adoption and synthesise the critical domains advancing the field. We screened 984 records from a stratified random sample of 8725 records identified through Web of Science and Scopus, retaining 654 publications, and surveyed the corresponding authors of 121 applied studies. AI-methods publications have outnumbered applied studies each year since 2023, yet only 9.1% (11 of 121) of surveyed researchers reported using AI in their applied work. Adoption emerged only after 2022 and was restricted to blank-frame filtering alone (9.1%) or paired with species classification (90.9%). Other AI-enabled tasks such as individual re-identification, behaviour classification and real-time alerting were absent from all 288 applied papers reviewed and survey responses. Their prevalence in applied work is unlikely to exceed 1.04% (97.5% upper bound). Most image-processing tools, blank filtering, species classification and real-time alerting are well developed; adoption in applied research lags, as expected for emerging technology. Implementation also hinges on two still-underdeveloped capacities: propagating classifier uncertainty into population estimates and automated camera-to-animal distance measurement for density estimation of unmarked populations. Bridging these gaps requires standardised reporting, interdisciplinary collaboration and uncertainty-aware workflows."
